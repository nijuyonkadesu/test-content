Reading Steiner is the name Okabe gave to his own anomalous ability: when a worldline shift occurs, his memories do not update with the rest of the universe. Everyone else seamlessly inherits the new history, complete with adjusted memories, photographs, and lunch receipts; Okabe keeps the old ones. He experiences a shift as a lurch of vertigo — "the world blinks" — followed by the slow, cold discovery of everything that is now different and always has been.

Without Reading Steiner there would be no wiki page for anything in this category, because no one would know any of it had happened. Every D-Mail's before-and-after, every undone timeline documented here, survives in exactly one place that isn't a Nixie tube. #lore

🌀Phenomenology

A shift, from inside Okabe's head: momentary dissociation (reported duration under two seconds), a sensation he describes as the visual field "tearing sideways without moving," then continuity — except the room may have changed, the person he was speaking to may be elsewhere, may not know him, or may never have existed. The old worldline's memories remain vivid and do not fade faster than ordinary memory.

The ability triggers on every confirmed shift, with no known off switch. This is documented in the lab notebook as a disability as much as a gift: after the worst stretch of the lab's first summer, Okabe experienced fifteen shifts in under a month and retained coherent, sequential memories of several timelines in which a lab member died. The handbook's rule that no one undertakes shift-heavy operations alone dates from that month.

Shift sensations, logged

  • Vertigo — 100% of shifts
  • Nausea — large shifts only
  • Sideways "tear" — 80%
  • Déjà vu inversion — described once, never explained
  • Headache — correlates with divergence delta

The partial cases cluster around emotionally extreme erased events, suggesting whatever carries memory across a shift couples to salience rather than recency. Kurisu's marginal note: "or the brain reconstructs from the same cues twice and we're pattern-matching grief. Both hypotheses fit. One is kinder."

🔬Hypotheses

Something in the bearer's brain fails to participate in the shift — a defect, in the same sense a surge protector is a defective wire. Predicts RS should be detectable on imaging. It isn't, or the lab lacks the machine that could.

Memory is state; a shift is a state overwrite; RS is a failed write. The SERN archives' memo speculating about "memory-carrier transfer" reads uncomfortably like an engineering spec for inducing exactly this. The lab does not enjoy this hypothesis.

Worldlines in which no one remembers shifts contain no one who documents shifts. A wiki about worldline shifts can only exist downstream of a Reading Steiner. This is either profound or a tautology, and the lab's standing vote is 3–3 with one abstention (Mayuri, who says it sounds sad either way).

Okabe has given four contradictory etymologies, involving respectively a physicist, a mystic, an anagram, and "it came to me in the shift, which means the worldline itself named it." The lab's official position, recorded in the handbook glossary: the name is load-bearing nonsense and must never be changed, as renaming things has historically gone poorly for this lab.

🛡️Operational doctrine

Because the bearer is the lab's only shift witness, standing procedure treats Okabe's testimony as an instrument reading: logged verbatim, dated, cross-checked against the meter photo, never argued with in the first hour. The doctrine's second clause is the humane one — after any shift, someone sits with him while he lists what changed, because the list is the debrief and the debrief is the cost.
