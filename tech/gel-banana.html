The Gel Banana Phenomenon is the lab's first piece of experimental evidence that the Phone Microwave was doing something to causality rather than to lunch. A banana placed in the modified microwave during a phone-linked cycle emerged transformed: its shape preserved, its substance converted to a uniform, faintly translucent green gel — at room temperature, with no cooking odor and no measurable heating. The lab's initial classification, "failed food experiment," survived exactly until someone noticed the second impossibility: the gelled banana was back on its bunch, across the room, reattached at the stem.

🔍The observation

Reconstruction from the lab notebook: a banana was placed in the drum as improvised organic ballast during a phone-mode test. The cycle ran nine seconds. The drum was empty afterward — noted at the time as "banana misplaced??" — and the bunch on the table, purchased that morning, had regrown its missing member as gel.

The sequence matters. The banana did not gel in the microwave; it vanished from the microwave and appeared, gelled, in the past position it had occupied before being picked from the bunch. The gel is what a thing looks like after being sent backward in time as matter, approximately one room and one hour, by a machine tuned for neither. Kurisu's later analysis put it flatly: the PhoneWave's first successful transmission wasn't a D-Mail. It was fruit.

The −4.2% mass deficit has never been accounted for. Applying $E = mc^2$ to 4.2 grams of banana yields $3.8 \times 10^{14}$ joules — which manifestly did not deposit in the lab, or there would be no lab, no Akihabara, and a substantially different wiki. Where the mass went is the phenomenon's real open question, and the lab's file on it is titled "DO NOT SOLVE THIS BY EXPERIMENT."

⚖️Significance

The gel bananas are the hinge of the lab's whole story: the moment its output changed from toys to evidence.

First reproducible temporal displacement of matter by the lab — and a perfect miniature of SERN's "jellification" wall. Matter goes back; matter arrives wrong. The lab reached in nine seconds and one banana the conclusion SERN's archives reach across three decades and fourteen human subjects, which is either a triumph of small science or the darkest data-efficiency argument on record.

Recognizing the gel as SERN's jellification is what let Kurisu redirect the lab toward information-only payloads — the insight that made D-Mail work. Every worldline shift documented on this wiki descends from someone looking at ruined fruit and taking it seriously.

Daru ate a spoonful of specimen #2 before the significance was understood. He reports the taste as "banana, but like it's remembering banana." No effects. He does not discuss it further, and the incident is the reason the handbook has a rule zero. See Rule Zero.

Three specimens persist in sealed jars on the lab's anomaly shelf, dated and initialed. Specimen #1 has desiccated into a translucent resin the color of old glass; #2 (bitten) is quarantined in a second, angrier jar; #3 remains gel-state and is the control nobody controls for anything. The Divergence Meter sits on the same shelf, which Mayuri arranged deliberately: "the warnings should live together." #lore
