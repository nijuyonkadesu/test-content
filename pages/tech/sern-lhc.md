The **SERN Large Hadron Collider** is the world's largest particle
accelerator and, according to archives no one was supposed to read, the
engine of a decades-long covert research program into time travel. Its
public output is respectable physics. Its private output — filed under the
internal designation **Z Program** — is a body of experimental reports on
using collider-generated micro black holes to send matter, and eventually
people, into the past.

The [[future-gadget-lab|Future Gadget Laboratory]] learned all of this the
hard way: SERN noticed the lab's D-Mails *first*, and [[daru|Daru]]'s
retaliatory dig through their network is what surfaced the Z Program at
all. See [[episode-04|Episode 04]] through [[episode-07|Episode 07]].

::section[The Z Program]{icon="🕳️"}

::::columns{ratio="60-40"}
:::column
The physics is disarmingly straightforward on paper. A collision at
sufficient energy density can, in principle, produce a microscopic black
hole. An electrically charged, rotating micro black hole — a **Kerr–Newman
singularity** — drags spacetime around itself, and a signal threaded past
its ring singularity on the right trajectory exits the loop *before* it
entered. SERN's contribution was not the theory but the engineering:
keeping such an object stable for longer than $10^{-27}$ seconds, which the
archives claim was first achieved in the early 2000s using what the reports
call an "exotic matter lining" whose composition is redacted even in the
decoded text. ==Hawking radiation should make this impossible==; the
reports acknowledge as much, then continue anyway.

The mass-energy bookkeeping is brutal. Compressing even one gram into its
Schwarzschild radius demands energies of order

$$E = mc^2 = (10^{-3}\,\text{kg})(3\times10^8\,\text{m/s})^2 = 9\times10^{13}\ \text{J}$$

which is why SERN needed the LHC — and why the lab's 40-joule
[[phone-microwave|PhoneWave]] achieving comparable results made SERN, in
the words of one intercepted memo, "极度不安" (the archives switch
languages when agitated). #lore
:::

::section[Human experiments]{icon="⚠️"}

:::danger
The decoded reports include a series titled *Human Is Dead, Mismatch* —
autopsy summaries of test subjects sent through the collider's temporal
aperture. Every subject arrived at their target time **spatially
compressed**, the archives' word for which translates as "jellified."
Every date of death *precedes* the date of transmission. The lab stopped
reading these aloud after the second one.
:::

The failure mode is a mismatch between the transmitted mass-energy and the
aperture's capacity: the LHC could send an object back in time, but not
*intact*. It is precisely this wall that made SERN so interested in the
lab's accidental success with information-only transmission — a D-Mail has
no mass to jellify.

| Report series | Years | Subjects | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Z-062 "displacement" | 1973–1981 | Inorganic | Position drift, partial arrival |
| Z-143 "organic trials" | 1982–1994 | Biological samples | Total cellular collapse |
| Z-201 "HID/M" | 1995–2004 | Human (14 known) | 100% fatal, retro-dated |
| Z-288 "aperture II" | 2005– | Ongoing | Redacted |

::section[Detection network]{icon="📡"}

SERN operates a worldwide monitoring net — nicknamed **ECHELON's weirder
sibling** by Daru — that flags electromagnetic events consistent with
temporal displacement. The lab's first D-Mails lit it up like a flare. The
network triangulated the source to [[akihabara|Akihabara]] within three
transmissions, which is roughly when unfamiliar vans began parking outside
the [[braun-tube-workshop|Braun Tube Workshop]].

::::tabs
:::tab{label="What it detects"}
Phase-inverted microwave bursts with negative group delay — the signature
of information arriving before it left. A [[time-leap-machine|time leap]]
is *much* quieter than a D-Mail, one reason the lab survived long enough to
matter.
:::
:::tab{label="What it missed"}
Anything not travelling by electromagnetic carrier. The archives betray
real anxiety about this blind spot, with one memo speculating about
"memory-carrier transfer" a full decade before the lab stumbled into
exactly that.
:::
::::
